WEEK 1

Chemical Reactions

Learning CompetencyDescribe the indicators of a chemical reaction, including gas production and temperature change.

Experiment: Observing Chemical Reactions: Vinegar and Baking Soda

Watch on YouTube ↗

ALearning objectives

At the end of the experiment, learners should be able to:

  1. Cognitive: Identify gas production and temperature change as observable indicators of a chemical reaction.
  2. Affective: Demonstrate appreciation for the importance of careful observation and safety when conducting chemical experiments.
  3. Psychomotor: Perform or observe the vinegar and baking soda experiment to identify evidence of a chemical reaction.

BLaboratory manual

Introduction

Chemical reactions occur when substances are transformed into new substances. These reactions can be recognized through observable indicators such as color change, formation of a precipitate, gas production, odor change, and temperature change.

In this experiment, vinegar (acetic acid) reacts with baking soda (sodium bicarbonate), producing carbon dioxide gas, water, and sodium acetate.

Materials

  • 50 mL white vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon baking soda
  • Clear plastic cup
  • Measuring spoon
  • Thermometer
  • Stirring stick
  • Safety goggles

Procedure

  1. Pour 50 mL of vinegar into a clear plastic cup.
  2. Measure and record the initial temperature of the vinegar.
  3. Observe and describe the appearance of the vinegar.
  4. Add one teaspoon of baking soda to the vinegar.
  5. Observe the reaction carefully, especially the formation of bubbles.
  6. After the reaction has settled, measure and record the final temperature.
  7. Complete the observation table.

Expected results

  • Bubbling and fizzing will occur.
  • Carbon dioxide gas will be released.
  • The temperature may decrease.
  • The mixture will show visible changes.

Chemical equation

NaHCO3 + CH3COOH → CH3COONa + H2O + CO2

WEEK 2

Acids, Bases, and Salts

Learning CompetencyIdentify common acids, bases, and salts using different indicators.

Experiment: Testing Acids and Bases Using Red Cabbage Indicator

Watch on YouTube ↗

ALearning objectives

At the end of the experiment, learners should be able to:

  1. Classify common household substances as acidic, basic, or approximately neutral based on indicator results.
  2. Value the importance of identifying acids and bases in understanding the properties and safe use of common substances.
  3. Prepare or observe the use of red cabbage indicator to test different solutions.

BLaboratory manual

Introduction

Acids, bases, and salts are common chemical substances found in everyday life. Indicators are used to identify whether a solution is acidic or basic.

Red cabbage contains anthocyanin pigments that change color depending on the pH of a solution.

In this experiment, learners will use red cabbage extract to test different household solutions.

Materials

  • Red cabbage extract
  • White vinegar
  • Lemon juice
  • Baking soda solution
  • Saline solution (table salt dissolved in water)
  • Water
  • Five clear cups
  • Droppers
  • Safety goggles

Procedure

  1. Prepare red cabbage extract by soaking or crushing red cabbage leaves in warm water, then filtering the liquid.
  2. Label five cups according to the solutions being tested.
  3. Place approximately 10 mL of each solution into its corresponding cup.
  4. Add approximately 5 mL of red cabbage indicator to each cup.
  5. Observe the color produced in each solution.
  6. Record the color changes in the observation table.
  7. Classify each solution as acidic, basic, or approximately neutral based on the observed color.

Expected results

SubstanceExpected colorClassification
VinegarPink/redAcidic
Lemon juicePink/redAcidic
Baking soda solutionBlue/greenBasic
Saline solutionPurpleApproximately neutral
WaterPurpleNeutral

Note: Actual colors depend on the concentration and pH of each solution.

Additional Lesson Discussion: Understanding Salts

A salt is an ionic compound commonly formed through reactions involving acids and bases. Sodium chloride (NaCl), or table salt, is a familiar example.

Although sodium chloride solution is generally close to neutral, not all salts produce neutral solutions. The properties of a salt solution depend on the ions present.

Connection to the competency: Discuss hydrochloric acid (HCl) as an example of an acid and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) as an example of a base. These should be identified through labels, formulas, and teacher-provided information rather than handled by learners in this household-material experiment.

WEEK 3

Types of Chemical Reactions

Learning CompetencyDescribe combination, decomposition, single replacement, and double replacement reactions.

Experiment: Formation of a Precipitate Through a Double Displacement Reaction

Watch on YouTube ↗

ALearning objectives

At the end of the experiment, learners should be able to:

  1. Differentiate the four common types of chemical reactions based on their characteristics.
  2. Develop curiosity about the chemical changes that occur in everyday materials and their surroundings.
  3. Record observations of a double displacement reaction using an observation sheet.

BLaboratory manual

Introduction

Chemical reactions can be classified according to how substances combine, separate, or exchange components.

The four major types include:

  • Combination reaction
  • Decomposition reaction
  • Single replacement reaction
  • Double replacement reaction

In this activity, learners will observe a double displacement reaction through the formation of a precipitate.

A precipitate is an insoluble solid that forms when two solutions react.

Materials

  • Epsom salt (magnesium sulfate)
  • Sodium carbonate (washing soda)
  • Water
  • Two clear cups
  • Stirring sticks
  • Measuring spoons
  • Safety goggles

Procedure

  1. Label the two clear cups.
  2. Pour approximately 25 mL of magnesium sulfate solution into the first cup.
  3. Pour approximately 25 mL of sodium carbonate solution into the second cup.
  4. Observe the initial appearance of both solutions.
  5. Slowly pour the magnesium sulfate solution into the sodium carbonate solution.
  6. Observe any changes in the mixture.
  7. Look for the formation of a white solid.
  8. Record the observations in the table.
  9. Identify the type of chemical reaction demonstrated.

Expected results

A white solid may form when the two solutions are combined. This solid is called a precipitate.

Chemical equation

MgSO4(aq) + Na2CO3(aq) → MgCO3(s) + Na2SO4(aq)

This is a double displacement reaction because the ions exchange partners, producing magnesium carbonate, a solid precipitate.

Safety: Use small quantities, avoid inhaling powders, wear goggles, and do proper disposal.

CAdditional learning material

The experiment demonstrates double displacement. To ensure that the competency covers all four reaction types, learners should also study the following examples in the lesson handout.

WEEK 4

Chemical Equations and Conservation of Mass

Learning CompetencyRecognize chemical equations, explain the rearrangement of atoms, and apply conservation of mass to chemical reactions.

Experiment: Investigation of the Conservation of Mass in a Closed System

Watch on YouTube ↗

ALearning objectives

At the end of the experiment, learners should be able to:

  1. Explain the law of conservation of mass in a chemical reaction.
  2. Demonstrate appreciation for accurate measurement and scientific evidence.
  3. Measure and record the mass of a reaction setup before and after a chemical reaction.

BLaboratory manual

Introduction

The Law of Conservation of Mass states that matter cannot be created or destroyed during an ordinary chemical reaction. The total mass of the reactants is equal to the total mass of the products in a closed system.

In this experiment, vinegar and baking soda will react inside a sealed resealable bag. The reaction produces carbon dioxide gas, which remains inside the system.

Materials

  • 20 mL white vinegar
  • Approximately 1 g baking soda
  • Erlenmeyer flask or suitable plastic bottle
  • Balloon
  • Funnel
  • Digital weighing scale
  • Safety goggles
  • Tray

Procedure

  1. Pour approximately 20 mL of vinegar into the flask.
  2. Using a funnel, place approximately 1 g of baking soda inside the balloon.
  3. Carefully attach the balloon to the mouth of the flask without allowing the baking soda to fall into the vinegar.
  4. Make sure the balloon is securely attached to prevent gas from escaping.
  5. Place the entire setup on the digital weighing scale.
  6. Record the initial mass.
  7. Lift the balloon so the baking soda falls into the vinegar.
  8. Observe the reaction as bubbles form and the balloon inflates.
  9. Wait until the reaction has stopped.
  10. Measure and record the final mass of the complete setup while the balloon remains attached.
  11. Compare the initial and final masses.

Safety reminders

  • Use a flexible balloon and suitable flask or bottle.
  • Do not use a rigid sealed container.
  • Do not overfill the balloon or flask.
  • Keep the setup away from the face.

Expected results

  • Bubbles form due to carbon dioxide production.
  • The bag may expand slightly.
  • The initial and final masses should be approximately equal if the bag remains sealed.
Safety reminder: Use a flexible freezer bag, not a rigid sealed container. Do not squeeze or overfill the bag. Perform the activity under teacher supervision.
